This was my group project for COMP3900 (Human-Computer Interaction), Semester 5, 2023, at ANU. We started from a different idea, a digital mental health support platform for international students, but as the discovery research and team discussions progressed, we pivoted toward a problem we could prototype more concretely within the course's design process: getting people on campus to actually notice and care about the wildlife around them.

The result was ARchive, an augmented-reality app where users scan native animals around the ANU campus, such as quokkas, koalas, brushtails, and green tree frogs, using their phone camera. Each scan rewards a collectible card with real biodiversity information (weight, length, conservation status, diet), and the card's hint nudges the user to attempt a harder scan next time, such as catching the same animal in its natural habitat or while feeding. Collecting a full set unlocks a species' wiki page. A virtual pet you feed with in-game currency, a friend leaderboard, and a shop where in-game gold can be redeemed for real prizes tied to ANU's actual biodiversity events round out the loop, turning passive noticing into a small habit.

My role centred on the prototyping pipeline. I built a low-fidelity version first, a hollow-carved semitransparent mock-up of a phone camera interface with paper animal cutouts, to test the core scan interaction cheaply before anyone touched Figma. I then worked on the high-fidelity Figma prototype and presented our demo script and walkthrough video for Assessment 2.

The course also ran a structured peer moderation process and a six-topic reflective learning log (Assessment 3), which pushed me to evaluate our own design against Jakob Nielsen's usability heuristics rather than just demo it. Doing that review surfaced a real gap: the prize-redeem flow processed an exchange instantly with no confirmation step, which conflicted with "user control and freedom." We fixed that before the final hi-fi build.

What stuck with me afterward is how much the paper prototype changed our thinking before we ever opened a design tool, and how a heuristic review caught a usability flaw that a normal walkthrough would have missed entirely. Low fidelity is for finding the right problem; high fidelity is for finding the small things you'd otherwise ship by accident.
